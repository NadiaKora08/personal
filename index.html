Senior design

Semi-automated blood pressure regulation

Senior design, University of South Florida, partnered with MiracleBots, LLC
August 2024 to May 2025 — team of three
ISO 14971 · ISO 13485 · ISO 81060-2 · IEC 60601-2-24

The three of us built a semi-automated blood pressure regulation system: a non-invasive cuff, a peristaltic pump that delivers nicardipine, and the software that connects them. It keeps a patient's pressure in range on its own, so a clinician does not have to reset the dose by hand every few minutes.

Wireless communication and code

Two boards, one client, and user interface between them

The system holds a patient's blood pressure where the clinician wants it, using the prescribed medication, without anyone adjusting the rate by hand. That helps in an emergency, and for patients who need fluids whenever their pressure drops, or medication to bring a high pressure down.

The cuff and the pump never talk to each other directly. Each one is an ESP32 running a BLE server, and a single Python application connects to both. It reads the pressure from the cuff, works out a dose, and sends the rate, duration and drug type to the pump.

Everything passes through that application, so a cuff reading cannot reach the pump on its own. The interface shows the measured pressure, the heart rate and a suggested rate, and nothing is delivered until the clinician confirms it.

I wrote the software across Python, C++ and MATLAB: the signal processing, the wireless communication, the safety checks in the code, and the hardware programming. The dosing model learns how an individual patient responds to a dose instead of following a fixed schedule.

Electronic components

Building the pump and the cuff

Each enclosure carries its own supply and control. A 24 V wall supply is bucked down to 5 V for the logic; an ESP32 Wroom holds the BLE link and drives a motor driver over RS485, which turns the peristaltic pump head. A micro-servo handles the medication switch.

The cuff side is the same idea with different actuators: 7.5 V bucked to 5 V, an ESP32 driving an H-bridge that runs a diaphragm air pump and a solenoid valve, and a pressure sensor tapped into the cuff line.Those two inflate the cuff and then let the air out at a controlled rate, since the measurement depends on how fast the pressure falls.

Signal processing

Recovering a pressure from a deflation curve

We measure pressure with the cuff indirectly: it inflates, the pressure falls slowly, and every heartbeat shows up as a small oscillation riding on that falling curve. I wrote the signal processing that separates those oscillations out with a bandpass filter and reads the systolic and diastolic values from the size of the pulses.

The filtering and the peak detection run on the signal as it arrives rather than on a saved recording, because the next dose depends on the reading that just came in.

Standards

Following ISO protocols to fix and validate

Four standards shaped the design, and each one changed something in the result.

ISO 14971
While working on our devices, we ran risk assessment and management that defined our choices in the design, even if this meant making the device more complicated.
ISO 13485
During the device development, we kept a design history and controlled every revision, so any requirement could be traced to the part of the device that satisfies it.
ISO 81060-2
We validated the cuff against this standard, which set how accurate the measurement had to be and the conditions it had to hold that accuracy under.
IEC 60601-2-24
We built the pump to this standard, which set the delivery accuracy, how the pump behaves when a line blocks, and when it has to stop delivering.

CAD & 3D-printing

Printed housings for both devices

We printed both enclosures ourselves, giving every component its own seat inside so nothing moves around and the clearances that medical electrical safety needs stay intact. We also added vents for cooling, a hole for the power inlet, routing for the tubing that connects to the IV line and the switch between lines, as well as the fixtures that clamp the system to a standard IV pole. The motor and the commercial pump head were seated outside the box, with their electrical connections inside.

We designed the first pump head ourselves, modelling the roller spacing and the tubing path that set how much fluid moves per turn, and then moved to a commercial head.

Morse-code IoT device workshop

LED and photosensor data transmission

I built this session around two Particle Argon boards that talk over a beam of visible light. The sender drives an LED from a digital pin, and the receiver that reads photocurrent from the photosensor as analog input and wires a second LED to follow the increases and decreses in the input.

Working in pairs, the student were tasked with building both components step-by-step, program their devices and troubleshoot to make their devices work and be able to replicate them in the future. The students then encoded and transmitted Morse code at the sender and decoded information on the receiver end, testing it with the room lights off.

Mobile Phone IoT Workshop

Talking from microphone to dynamic over WiFi

The second workshop moves to audio: two ESP32 boards carrying sound between them over I²S, with a MEMS microphone on one end and a Class-D amplifier driving a speaker on the other.

Students built both ends, programmed the boards, and worked through the I²S interface: bit clock, word select, and how a sample stream is framed. By the end each pair could talk into their microphone and hear it come out of the speaker across the room.

Solidworksa and FDM printing

Building Minecraft cat CAD

I built this session around a Minecraft cat model in SOLIDWORKS. The build goes through the basic CAD functions one at a time — sketching, extrusion, and the buttons students keep coming back to. Working along with me, the students were tasked with modelling the cat step-by-step, so they could repeat the process on their own parts later. We then went through the slicer software, SLA and FDM printing and what each is good for, and the resources available to them on campus and online.

Junior design

Wireless EOG drowsiness monitor

Junior design, University of South Florida
April to May 2024
Top three of fifteen teams, 2024 Florida Medical Manufacturing Consortium

BA wearable that reads eye movement through electrodes on the face, counts blinks, and flags drowsiness. We built it for truck drivers, where fatigue is behind a share of fatal crashes and blink rate is a validated marker of it.

EOG Signal

Leveraging ocular muscle biopotential

Electrooculography reads the standing potential between the cornea and the retina, about 6 mV, so every eye movement and blink shows up directly in the signal. We put the inverting input above the eyebrow, the non-inverting input beside the outer eye, and the reference on the jaw.

The acquisition circuit is a passive bandpass filter with cutoffs at 0.72 and 15.9 Hz feeding an instrumentation amplifier, with the output offset between 0 and 5 V so the Arduino could read it. Two 9 V batteries give the split supply, and a buzzer and indicator LED sit on the board for the alert.

Communication and Code

Bluetooth data transfer to mobile app

The Arduino code detects the peaks in the EOG and PPG traces and derives the blink and pulse frequencies from them, with a fourth-order Butterworth filter smoothing the signal before detection. The device sends the result over Bluetooth to a phone app I built in MIT App Inventor, which shows the blink events as they arrive and the current awake or drowsy state. We added PPG so the drowsiness reading has a second signal behind it rather than resting on blink count alone.
