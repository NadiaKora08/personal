Two device programmes, and the fabrication, electronics and modelling skills that both of them run on.

01 — Cleanroom fabrication

Processing at wafer scale

Both of my lab projects live or die on the cleanroom process behind them. I develop the process, qualify it, and then run it at volume — photolithography and mask design in L-Edit, mould production, casting and release, and the inspection step that decides whether a batch is usable.

For the MAP patch that meant more than a hundred devices across design iterations. Building at that count is what made the design converge: each batch surfaced yield and assembly problems that were invisible in single prototypes.

02 — 3D printing and CAD

From parametric sketch to released part

Device housings, moulds, fixtures and alignment jigs all start as SOLIDWORKS models. I design them parametrically so a dimension change propagates instead of forcing a redraw, and I print them in-house — FDM for fixtures and fit checks, SLA where the feature size or the surface finish demands it.

The microneedle array is the clearest case: needle pitch, height and the registration cut-outs are all driven parameters, because every fabrication round moved at least one of them.

03 — Electrical troubleshooting

Measuring the device instead of reasoning about it

Early MAP builds drew far more power than a wearable format allows. Finding out why meant instrumenting the drive circuitry rather than reading the schematic harder — current draw per emitter, duty cycle, and where the losses actually sat.

The redesigned drive consumes two orders of magnitude less power than the first build. That result came out of measurement, and it is the habit I would carry into any device programme.

04 — Thermal control

Thirty degrees of headroom

A patch worn against a wound has a hard temperature ceiling, and the first build ran far past it. The thermal path and the electrical design turned out to be the same problem seen from two directions — the emitters were being driven in a way that dumped heat exactly where the device touches skin.

Reworking both together took 30 °C off the peak. That is what moved the concept from a bench demonstration to something wearable.

05 — Time optimization

Cutting a two-day build to two hours

The first perfusion chamber worked, but took two full days to fabricate. That made iteration impossibly slow: any design question cost two days to answer, so most questions went unasked.

Reworking the microfabrication process brought the build down to two hours. It did not make the device better directly — it made the device able to be wrong and get fixed, and the design has been through many more revisions since as a direct result. On a project with three interacting subsystems, iteration speed turned out to be the real constraint.

06 — Modelling and design

Checking it before it is machined

The perfusion chamber is a fluidics problem constrained by an optical one: the brain slice has to stay accessible to light and to a probe while the flow field over it stays uniform. Modelling the velocity field is how that uniformity gets checked before anything is cut — the flow equalisers exist because early geometries put a gradient straight across the tissue.

07 — In vitro and in vivo validation

Showing that it does something

A device that runs cool and draws little power still has to work. The MAP patch went through in vitro validation with fibroblasts — irradiance characterised with and without the microlens array, then scratch and viability assays showing accelerated closure and increased proliferation.

Those results were strong enough to write up within the first month of the project. The device has since advanced through in vivo validation.

08 — Publishing

First author at IEEE MEMS 2026

The work became a first-author conference paper and poster one month after I joined the lab: device concept, design and fabrication, irradiance characterisation, and the in vitro assays.

Teaching embedded hardware by building devices that only work if you understand why.

01 — Optical Morse link

An LED that listens

Two Particle Argon boards talking over a beam of visible light. The sender drives an LED from a digital pin; the receiver wires a second LED in reverse and reads the photocurrent on an analog input, which works because an LED is a diode with a junction that responds to light as readily as it emits it.

Using an LED as its own detector is what makes the session teach something. It forces the question of what the component actually is, rather than what the tutorial calls it, and it lets students see a real analog signal instead of a boolean. Pairs sent Morse across a table, then tested it with the room lights off.

02 — Two-way Wi-Fi audio

Framing a sample stream

A pair of ESP32 boards carrying live audio between them: a MEMS microphone on one end and a Class-D amplifier on the other, both over I²S. The session works through the interface properly — bit clock, word select, and how a sample stream is framed — because those are the concepts that transfer to any synchronous digital bus a student meets later.

03 — CAD and 3D printing

From blank sketch to printed part

A separate track takes students from a blank SOLIDWORKS sketch to a printed part: parametric modelling, STL and 3MF export, slicer configuration, supports and resolution, and choosing between FDM and SLA for a given part. Most attendees have never printed anything before and leave having sent a file to a printer themselves.

04 — Curriculum

Built backwards from a device that has to work

Most students meet embedded hardware through a tutorial they can follow without understanding. I build the workshops backwards from that: a device that only works if you understand why. I design and teach the whole curriculum — the circuits, the firmware, the slides, and the debugging that happens when thirty breadboards fail in thirty different ways.

Sessions run in pairs so nobody watches someone else do the build. Over 200 attendees so far, and more than 100 devices built.

04 — Senior design

Semi-automated blood pressure regulation

Senior design, University of South Florida, partnered with MiracleBots, LLC
August 2024 to May 2025 — team of three
ISO 14971 · ISO 13485 · ISO 81060-2 · IEC 60601-2-24

Titrating a vasoactive drug by hand is slow, and the patient moves between readings. This system closes the loop and keeps the clinician in charge of it.

01 — The problem

The lag between readings

ICU patients on nicardipine need their blood pressure held inside a narrow window. In practice a nurse measures, adjusts the infusion rate, waits, and measures again. The lag between those steps is where the patient drifts out of range, and the workload scales badly across a ward.

Three of us built the whole system from nothing: a non-invasive oscillometric cuff, a peristaltic pump for drug delivery, and the software connecting them.

02 — Wireless communication

Two boards and a laptop in one loop

The cuff and the pump never talk to each other directly. Each is an ESP32 running a BLE GATT server, and the Python application is the single client that connects to both — it subscribes to pressure notifications from the cuff, computes a dose, and writes rate, duration and drug type to the pump as a packed characteristic.

Putting the application in the middle is a safety decision as much as an architectural one: there is no path by which a cuff reading reaches the pump without passing through the software that a clinician has authority over.

03 — Code and the clinician GUI

The interface holds the authority

I owned the software stack across Python, C++ and MATLAB. The clinician application surfaces the computed pressure, heart rate and a suggested nicardipine rate — but the dose is never applied until a clinician commits it. The loop is deliberately semi-automated, not autonomous.

The control side models how an individual patient responds to a dose rather than applying a fixed schedule, using machine learning over the measured response.

04 — Signal processing

Recovering a pressure from a deflation curve

Blood pressure is not measured directly. The cuff is inflated and bled down, and the pressure trace is bandpass filtered to isolate the oscillometric pulses riding on the decay. Systolic and diastolic values are then read off fixed ratios of the peak envelope.

Real-time filtering, peak detection and the ratio logic all run on the incoming stream rather than on a stored recording, because the next dose decision depends on the reading that just arrived.

05 — Hardware and CAD

Both devices, from model to build

I designed and built the CAD and hardware for the cuff and the pump: enclosures, the peristaltic pump mechanism and its drive, and the mounting that puts the whole system on a standard IV pole.

06 — Designing to standards

Each standard, and what it changed

This was the project where regulatory work stopped being abstract. Four standards shaped the design, and each one is visible in the result.

ISO 14971
Risk management ran alongside the design rather than after it. Several design choices exist because a hazard analysis asked for them, not because they were the neatest engineering answer.
ISO 13485
Quality system practice — design history, controlled revisions, and traceability from a requirement to the thing that satisfies it.
ISO 81060-2
Set what the non-invasive cuff had to demonstrate, and therefore what the human subject protocol had to measure.
IEC 60601-2-24
Governed the infusion side: pump accuracy, occlusion behaviour, and the conditions under which delivery has to stop.

07 — Validation

Getting a student build to human testing

We took the cuff through IRB-approved testing on human subjects, which meant the protocol, the consent process and the data handling all had to hold up before a single measurement was taken. Getting a student-built device to that point was most of the work.

05 — Junior design

Wireless EOG drowsiness monitor

Junior design, University of South Florida
April to May 2024
Top three of fifteen teams, 2024 Florida Medical Manufacturing Consortium

Blink rate is a validated marker of sleepiness. A wearable that measures it can warn a driver before the driver notices.

01 — Biopotential sensing

Reading the eye directly

Electrooculography measures the corneo-retinal potential, so eye movement and blinks appear directly in the signal. Blink frequency is a clinically validated sleepiness marker, which makes it a reasonable thing to monitor continuously in a driver — the measurement is unobtrusive and the signal is large enough to work with outside a lab.

02 — Wireless and app

BLE to a phone, readable at a glance

The device acquires the EOG signal, extracts blink events, and streams the result over Bluetooth Low Energy to a paired phone application. I built the app and the full BLE communication layer in C++, with an interface designed to be readable at a glance — anything demanding attention defeats the purpose of a drowsiness warning.
