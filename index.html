Measured, not assumed

Early MAP builds ran far too hot against skin and drew far more power than a wearable allows. I found out why by measuring the device under drive rather than re-reading the schematic — temperature logged live, current per emitter, duty cycle.

The redesign took 30 °C off the peak and two orders of magnitude off the power.

02 — Senior design

Semi-automated blood pressure regulation

Senior design, University of South Florida, partnered with MiracleBots, LLC
August 2024 to May 2025 — team of three
ISO 14971 · ISO 13485 · ISO 81060-2 · IEC 60601-2-24

Titrating a vasoactive drug by hand is slow, and the patient moves between readings. This system closes the loop and keeps the clinician in charge of it. Three of us built the whole thing from nothing: a non-invasive oscillometric cuff, a peristaltic pump for nicardipine delivery, and the software connecting them.

01 — Wireless communication and code

Two boards, one client, and the interface between them

The cuff and the pump never talk to each other directly. Each is an ESP32 running a BLE GATT server, and the Python application is the single client that connects to both — it subscribes to pressure notifications from the cuff, computes a dose, and writes rate, duration and drug type to the pump as a packed characteristic.

Putting the application in the middle is a safety decision as much as an architectural one: there is no path by which a cuff reading reaches the pump without passing through the software that a clinician has authority over. The interface surfaces the computed pressure, heart rate and a suggested rate, but nothing is delivered until a clinician commits it.

I owned the software stack across Python, C++ and MATLAB. The control side models how an individual patient responds to a dose rather than applying a fixed schedule, using machine learning over the measured response.

02 — Electronic components

What is inside each box

Each enclosure carries its own supply and control. A 24 V wall supply is bucked down to 5 V for the logic; an ESP32 Wroom holds the BLE link and drives a motor driver over RS485, which turns the peristaltic pump head. A micro-servo handles the medication switch.

The cuff side is the same idea with different actuators: 7.5 V bucked to 5 V, an ESP32 driving an H-bridge that runs a diaphragm air pump and a solenoid valve, and a pressure sensor tapped into the cuff line. Those two inflate the cuff and bleed it at a controlled rate, since the deflation slope is what the measurement depends on.

03 — Signal processing

Recovering a pressure from a deflation curve

Blood pressure is not measured directly. The cuff is inflated and bled down, and the pressure trace is bandpass filtered to isolate the oscillometric pulses riding on the decay. Systolic and diastolic values are then read off fixed ratios of the peak envelope.

Real-time filtering, peak detection and the ratio logic all run on the incoming stream rather than on a stored recording, because the next dose decision depends on the reading that just arrived.

04 — Standards

Each standard, and what it changed

This was the project where regulatory work stopped being abstract. Four standards shaped the design, and each one is visible in the result.

ISO 14971
Risk management ran alongside the design rather than after it. Several design choices exist because a hazard analysis asked for them, not because they were the neatest engineering answer.
ISO 13485
Quality system practice — design history, controlled revisions, and traceability from a requirement to the thing that satisfies it.
ISO 81060-2
Set what the non-invasive cuff had to demonstrate: the accuracy the measurement has to reach, and the conditions it has to reach it under.
IEC 60601-2-24
Governed the infusion side: pump accuracy, occlusion behaviour, and the conditions under which delivery has to stop.

05 — 3D printing

Printed housings for both devices

Both enclosures are printed, and so are the internal brackets that seat the boards, the solenoid and the diaphragm pump. The brackets exist so components land in a repeatable place rather than wherever the loom pulls them, which matters once a box has been opened and closed a few dozen times.

The first pump was ours: modelled from the roller geometry outward, since roller spacing and the tubing path set how much fluid moves per revolution. We moved to a commercial pump head after that, and the printed work became the housing and the mounting that puts the system on a standard IV pole.

Teaching embedded hardware by building devices that only work if you understand why.

01 — Optical Morse link

An LED that listens

Two Particle Argon boards talking over a beam of visible light. The sender drives an LED from a digital pin; the receiver wires a second LED in reverse and reads the photocurrent on an analog input, which works because an LED is a diode with a junction that responds to light as readily as it emits it.

Using an LED as its own detector is what makes the session teach something. It forces the question of what the component actually is, rather than what the tutorial calls it, and it lets students see a real analog signal instead of a boolean. Pairs sent Morse across a table, then tested it with the room lights off.

02 — Two-way Wi-Fi audio

Framing a sample stream

A pair of ESP32 boards carrying live audio between them: a MEMS microphone on one end and a Class-D amplifier on the other, both over I²S. The session works through the interface properly — bit clock, word select, and how a sample stream is framed — because those are the concepts that transfer to any synchronous digital bus a student meets later.

03 — CAD and FDM printing

From blank sketch to printed part

A separate track takes students from a blank SOLIDWORKS sketch to a printed part: parametric modelling, STL and 3MF export, slicer configuration, supports and resolution, and choosing between FDM and SLA for a given part. Most attendees have never printed anything before and leave having sent a file to a printer themselves.

04 — Curriculum

Built backwards from a device that has to work

Most students meet embedded hardware through a tutorial they can follow without understanding. I build the workshops backwards from that: a device that only works if you understand why. I design and teach the whole curriculum — the circuits, the firmware, the slides, and the debugging that happens when thirty breadboards fail in thirty different ways.

Sessions run in pairs so nobody watches someone else do the build. Over 200 attendees so far, and more than 100 devices built.

05 — Junior design

Wireless EOG drowsiness monitor

Junior design, University of South Florida
April to May 2024
Top three of fifteen teams, 2024 Florida Medical Manufacturing Consortium

Blink rate is a validated marker of sleepiness. A wearable that measures it can warn a driver before the driver notices.

01 — Biopotential sensing

Reading the eye directly

Electrooculography measures the corneo-retinal potential, so eye movement and blinks appear directly in the signal. Blink frequency is a clinically validated sleepiness marker, which makes it a reasonable thing to monitor continuously in a driver — the measurement is unobtrusive and the signal is large enough to work with outside a lab.

02 — Wireless and app

BLE to a phone, readable at a glance

The device acquires the EOG signal, extracts blink events, and streams the result over Bluetooth Low Energy to a paired phone application. I built the app and the full BLE communication layer in C++, with an interface designed to be readable at a glance — anything demanding attention defeats the purpose of a drowsiness warning.
