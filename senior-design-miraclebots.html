Semi-automated blood pressure regulation

Titrating a vasoactive drug by hand is slow, and the patient moves between readings. This system closes the loop and keeps the clinician in charge of it.

Senior design, University of South Florida, partnered with MiracleBots, LLC
August 2024 to May 2025 — team of three
ISO 14971 · ISO 13485 · ISO 81060-2 · IEC 60601-2-24

01 — The problem

The lag between readings

ICU patients on nicardipine need their blood pressure held inside a narrow window. In practice a nurse measures, adjusts the infusion rate, waits, and measures again. The lag between those steps is where the patient drifts out of range, and the workload scales badly across a ward.

Three of us built the whole system from nothing: a non-invasive oscillometric cuff, a peristaltic pump for drug delivery, and the software connecting them.

02 — Wireless communication

Two boards and a laptop in one loop

The cuff and the pump never talk to each other directly. Each is an ESP32 running a BLE GATT server, and the Python application is the single client that connects to both — it subscribes to pressure notifications from the cuff, computes a dose, and writes rate, duration and drug type to the pump as a packed characteristic.

Putting the application in the middle is a safety decision as much as an architectural one: there is no path by which a cuff reading reaches the pump without passing through the software that a clinician has authority over.

03 — Code and the clinician GUI

The interface holds the authority

I owned the software stack across Python, C++ and MATLAB. The clinician application surfaces the computed pressure, heart rate and a suggested nicardipine rate — but the dose is never applied until a clinician commits it. The loop is deliberately semi-automated, not autonomous.

The control side models how an individual patient responds to a dose rather than applying a fixed schedule, using machine learning over the measured response.

04 — Signal processing

Recovering a pressure from a deflation curve

Blood pressure is not measured directly. The cuff is inflated and bled down, and the pressure trace is bandpass filtered to isolate the oscillometric pulses riding on the decay. Systolic and diastolic values are then read off fixed ratios of the peak envelope.

Real-time filtering, peak detection and the ratio logic all run on the incoming stream rather than on a stored recording, because the next dose decision depends on the reading that just arrived.

05 — Hardware and CAD

Both devices, from model to build

I designed and built the CAD and hardware for the cuff and the pump: enclosures, the peristaltic pump mechanism and its drive, and the mounting that puts the whole system on a standard IV pole.

06 — Designing to standards

Each standard, and what it changed

This was the project where regulatory work stopped being abstract. Four standards shaped the design, and each one is visible in the result.

ISO 14971
Risk management ran alongside the design rather than after it. Several design choices exist because a hazard analysis asked for them, not because they were the neatest engineering answer.
ISO 13485
Quality system practice — design history, controlled revisions, and traceability from a requirement to the thing that satisfies it.
ISO 81060-2
Set what the non-invasive cuff had to demonstrate, and therefore what the human subject protocol had to measure.
IEC 60601-2-24
Governed the infusion side: pump accuracy, occlusion behaviour, and the conditions under which delivery has to stop.

07 — Validation

Getting a student build to human testing

We took the cuff through IRB-approved testing on human subjects, which meant the protocol, the consent process and the data handling all had to hold up before a single measurement was taken. Getting a student-built device to that point was most of the work.
