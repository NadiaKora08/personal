Acousto-Bioelectronics Lab

Two device programmes, and the fabrication, electronics and modelling skills that both of them run on.

Graduate Research Engineer — Dr. Albert Kim, University of South Florida
May 2025 to present — project lead on both programmes
MAP phototherapy patch · microfluidic brain slice perfusion chamber

01 — Cleanroom fabrication

Processing at wafer scale

Both of my lab projects live or die on the cleanroom process behind them. I develop the process, qualify it, and then run it at volume — photolithography and mask design in L-Edit, mould production, casting and release, and the inspection step that decides whether a batch is usable.

For the MAP patch that meant more than a hundred devices across design iterations. Building at that count is what made the design converge: each batch surfaced yield and assembly problems that were invisible in single prototypes.

02 — 3D printing and CAD

From parametric sketch to released part

Device housings, moulds, fixtures and alignment jigs all start as SOLIDWORKS models. I design them parametrically so a dimension change propagates instead of forcing a redraw, and I print them in-house — FDM for fixtures and fit checks, SLA where the feature size or the surface finish demands it.

The microneedle array is the clearest case: needle pitch, height and the registration cut-outs are all driven parameters, because every fabrication round moved at least one of them.

03 — Electrical troubleshooting

Measuring the device instead of reasoning about it

Early MAP builds drew far more power than a wearable format allows. Finding out why meant instrumenting the drive circuitry rather than reading the schematic harder — current draw per emitter, duty cycle, and where the losses actually sat.

The redesigned drive consumes two orders of magnitude less power than the first build. That result came out of measurement, and it is the habit I would carry into any device programme.

04 — Thermal control

Thirty degrees of headroom

A patch worn against a wound has a hard temperature ceiling, and the first build ran far past it. The thermal path and the electrical design turned out to be the same problem seen from two directions — the emitters were being driven in a way that dumped heat exactly where the device touches skin.

Reworking both together took 30 °C off the peak. That is what moved the concept from a bench demonstration to something wearable.

05 — Time optimization

Cutting a two-day build to two hours

The first perfusion chamber worked, but took two full days to fabricate. That made iteration impossibly slow: any design question cost two days to answer, so most questions went unasked.

Reworking the microfabrication process brought the build down to two hours. It did not make the device better directly — it made the device able to be wrong and get fixed, and the design has been through many more revisions since as a direct result. On a project with three interacting subsystems, iteration speed turned out to be the real constraint.

06 — Modelling and design

Checking it before it is machined

The perfusion chamber is a fluidics problem constrained by an optical one: the brain slice has to stay accessible to light and to a probe while the flow field over it stays uniform. Modelling the velocity field is how that uniformity gets checked before anything is cut — the flow equalisers exist because early geometries put a gradient straight across the tissue.

07 — In vitro and in vivo validation

Showing that it does something

A device that runs cool and draws little power still has to work. The MAP patch went through in vitro validation with fibroblasts — irradiance characterised with and without the microlens array, then scratch and viability assays showing accelerated closure and increased proliferation.

Those results were strong enough to write up within the first month of the project. The device has since advanced through in vivo validation.

08 — Publishing

First author at IEEE MEMS 2026

The work became a first-author conference paper and poster one month after I joined the lab: device concept, design and fabrication, irradiance characterisation, and the in vitro assays.
